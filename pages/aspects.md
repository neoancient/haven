# Aspects
Many game systems use a system of skills used to modify dice rolls to determine whether an action succeeds or fails.
While skill in the Fate system are sometimes used that way, they are more often used to interact with the setting in
a narrative way, to add story elements or to add flavor. This is done primarily through aspects.

At the most basic level, an aspect is simply a description of what makes someone or something or somewhere distinct.
Characters have aspects that describe who they are (as opposed to skills, which describe what they do). Scenes have
aspects such as _Poorly Lit_ or _Under Surveillance_. Items can have aspects as well. A generic hovercar may not
have any, but a memorable one might have aspects such as _Quick off the Line_ or _Pulls to the Left_. Even the campaign
itself has aspects.

Aspects interact with the game mechanics in three ways:
- A player can invoke a relevant aspect to get a bonus on a skill check or reroll the dice. This could be one of their
own aspects, a scene or campaign aspect, an item aspect, or even another character's aspect to use it against them. This
requires spending a fate point. When invoking another character's aspect against them, the other character receives a
fate point.
- A player can make a skill check to create an advantage, with the result that a new aspect is added to the scene.
Creating an advantage can also be used to discover an aspect of a participant in the scene or create a temporary one.
This usually comes with the ability to invoke the aspect once without spending a fate point.
- The GM can compel an aspect, which adds a complication to the story. The player whose aspect is being compelled can
refuse the compel by paying a fate point, or accept the compel, which gives the character a fate point. Compelling an
aspect never forces a player into any particular course of action, but establishes consequences for the character's
actions.

# Character Aspects
A player will determine their character's aspects during character creation. The first aspect is the character's
**High Concept**. This is a short phrase that sums up who the character is what their calling is. You might start with
something simple, then add flavor to make it more distinctive.

Examples:
- Generic: _**Wizard**_
- Add a twist: _**Renegade Wizard**_
- Add some detail
  - Add a connection that suggests a backstory: _**Renegade Wizard that Defied the Eternal Throne**_
  - Infuse personality: _**Renegade Wizard with a Talent for Breaking Rules**_

- Generic: _**Bounty Hunter**_
- Add a twist: _**Bounty Hunter with a Soft Spot**_
- Add some detail
  - Add some clarification: _**Bounty Hunter Who Can't Say No to a Sad Story**_
  - Add connections to the setting: _**Freehold Bounty Hunter Who Can't Walk Away from a Lost Cause**_

The second aspect is the character's **Trouble**. The trouble is what complicates the character's life, and is often the
reason they are part of the story.

Examples:
Our renegade wizard is on the run from somebody, and their enemies are an obvious choice for a Trouble. We might also
explore the reasons they went renegade. Betrayal? A matter of conscience? A lust after power?
- _**Pursued by the Archons**_
- _**Drawn to Power**_
- _**Guilt Over Those I Betrayed**_

Our bounty hunter also has some conflict built into the high concept. For the trouble we could go with internal conflict,
with jobs repeatedly pushing them to make tough choices; or external conflict, earning a bad reputation and losing the
trust of clients.
- _**Can't Cash in on the Desparate**_
- _**Too Soft for the Work**_
- _**Word's Out: He's a Sucker for a Story**_

The **High Concept** and the **Trouble** are relatively fixed, and only change when driven to by major shifts in the story.
The three remaining aspects are more flexible, and can be changed (with GM approval) at certain milestones. See
[Advancement]({{ site.baseurl }}/advancement.html).

Some approaches to defining aspects:
- A relationship: _**My Sister's Stuck in an Imperial Reeducation Camp**_, _**Hunter by My Former Crew**_, _**Protect My
Little Sister at All Costs**_
- A belief: _**Always Help the Little Guy**_, _**Machines Have Souls**_, _**The Empire Must Fall, No Matter the Cost**_
- A catchphrase: _**If It Sparks, I Can Fix It**_, _**Orders? I Thought Those Were Suggestions**_, _**More Means Better**_
- A descriptor: _**Grizzled Spacer with One Good Eye**_, _**Steel-Nerved**_, _**Quiet Until the Blasters Come Out**_
- A possession: _**Staff of Old Yrth Oak**_, _**Ancestral Armor Reforged with Tech**_, _**Sword Blessed by Three
Witches and a Drunk Priest**_
- Generic: _**Too Clever for His Own Good**_, _**Living Between Factions, Loyal to None**_, _**Haunted by a Past Astral
Drop Gone Wrong**_

The best aspects are double-edged. They should provide opportunities to invoke them for an advantage, as well as
opportunities for the GM to compel them. Invoking lets the characters be awesome, and compulsions make the story
interesting. One test of an aspect is to think of three situations in which it could come up in play. If you can't think
of at least one favorable and one unfavorable, you may want to work on it a bit more.

Discuss aspects with the GM and make sure you have the same understanding. Your aspects are one way of signaling to
the GM what kind of situations you want to be involved in. If you have something as part of your backstory but you are
not interested in having it come up frequently in the story, do not make it an aspect.

# Situation Aspects
Many games have rules that provide modifiers to die rolls in certain situations. In the fate system, these are handled
by situation aspects. A scene usually has a few aspects that provide some color, and character actions can create or
discover other aspects. These can be invoked for an advantage or compelled to complicate things just as any other aspect.
Examples:
- Physical features:
  - _**Low Gravity**_
  - _**Cover Is Sparse**_
  - _**Shower of Sparks from Power Conduits**_
- Positioning:
  - _**Catwalks Around the Perimeter**_
  - _**Line of Sight Obstructed**_
  - _**One Way In, One Way Out**_
- Obstacles:
  - _**Sealed Bulkhead Door**_
  - _**Radiation Levels Spiking**_
  - _**Maze of Shipping Containers**_
- Context:
  - _**So Many Security Cameras**_
  - _**Station Crew Overworked**_
  - _**Stardrive Offline**_
- Character status:
  - _**Pinned Down Behind Crates**_
  - _**Off-Balance**_
  - _**Flustered**_

# Consequences
A **Consequence** is an aspect that is taken on to reduce physical or mental damage so that the character can absorb
stress without being taken out. Consequences are temporary, but last longer than a scene. Consequences may also be taken
in certain other situations, such as a major cost for turned a failed **Overcome** action into a success. A character
whose action produces the consequence gets a one free invocation. For more on damage and consequences, see
[Actions and Conflict]({{ site.baseurl }}/actions_and_conflict.html).

# Boosts
A boost is a transient consequence. It can only be invoked once, after which it is removed from the scene. Boosts
can be generated by a **Create and Advantage** or **Attack** action that ties the target number, or by an **Attack** or
**Defend** action that succeeds with style. For more, see [Actions and Conflict]({{ site.baseurl }}/actions_and_conflict.html).

# Invoking Aspects
The primary way that characters interact with aspects is by _invoking_ them. To invoke an aspect, explain why it bears
on the situation, then do one of the following:
- Add +2 to the result of a skill check after the dice are rolled.
- Reroll all the dice from a skill check.
- Pass a +2 to someone else's skill check if it makes sense that your aspect would assist them in some way.
- Add +2 to passive resistance (another character makes a skill check against a fixed target number).

Invoking an aspect costs one fate point, unless you have a free invocation from creating the aspect. You can invoke as
many aspects as you want to on a single action, as long as you can explain why the aspect applies and have enough fate
points. You cannot invoke the same aspect multiple times on the same action, even if you can make a case for it to
apply in different ways. When applying multiple aspects, you can choose different effects. For example, you may choose
to reroll the dice, and then apply a +2. It is not necessary to decide whether to invoke an aspect before rolling the dice.
In fact it is often beneficial to wait until the dice are rolled before deciding whether to invoke an aspect.

When invoking an aspect on another character, that character receives the spent fate point, as if the aspect was
compelled.

# Creating and Discovering Aspects
When using a skill to create an advantage, the player can add an aspect to a scene or discover an existing aspect. A
success reveals the aspect and grants the character a free invocation. Success with Style grants two free invocations,
which a tie grants a **Boost** instead. Failure can result either in a failure to create the aspect or someone else
receiving a free invocation, as long as it is disadvantageous to the character, at the GM's discretion.

# Free Invocations
Free invocations of aspects are usually the result of creating or discovering an aspect through a **Create an Advantage**
action or an attack action that results in the target taking a consequence. Boosts also give a free invocation. A free
invocation can only be used by the character that created it, unless that character passes the free invocation to another
character, or the rules for the situation state otherwise. A free invocation has the same affect as a standard invocation,
except it does not require payment of a fate point. Also unlike standard invocations, free invocations can be stacked with
each other or with a standard invocation, allowing the same aspect to be invoked multiple times on the same action. For
instance, a character may use a free invocation on an aspect while also paying a fate point to invoke the aspect normally.
If the character has multiple free invocations on the aspect, any number of them can be used together, with or without
a normal invocation. A **Boost** only has a single free invocation, after which it vanishes from the scene, and may
not be invoked normally by anybody.

# Compelling Aspects
Compelling aspects is a major tool in the GM's toolbox for driving the plot. Players can influence the direction of the
action through selecting aspects that suggest the types of trouble they want to get into, and by suggesting to the GM
ways in which their aspects might lead to a complication.

To compel an aspect, the GM names the aspect and suggests a way it might add a story complication. The player may
negotiate the nature of the complication, and the GM should be open to the player's suggestions about the direction of
the story. At a point when they come to an agreement or the GM makes the final offer, the player must decide whether
to accept the compulsion or refuse it. If the player accepts, they receive a fate point. To refuse the compel, they must
pay a fate point. A player that does not have any fate points may not refuse a compel.

Players should not be too hasty in refusing compels. While they may do it any time they have fate points, the system of
compelling aspects ensures that it is the character's flaws and difficulties that move the story forward. The reward
of a fate point gives the character a chance to be awesome in getting out of the complication. And a story with
no complications is boring.

# The Fate Point Economy
Fate points are used to power character actions through invocations and enabling certain stunts, and to drive the
story through compels. A good game session should see fate points moving back and forth between the players and the GM. If the fate points
tend to sit in the same pile, it suggests that the characters and the story are not engaging with each other.
