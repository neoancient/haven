# Magic

The ability to convince the universe to bend the rules and behave a little differently in a small area for a brief moment
is known colloquially as magic. There are different ways for characters to gain access to the ability to use magic, but the
mechanics of how magic works is the same whether it is acquired through study, granted by a supernatural patron, or intrinsic
to one's existence.

## Acquiring Magical Ability
The most common way of adding the ability to use magic to a character is to spend a point of refresh to get access to a
realm of magic. In order to do this, the character must have an aspect related to their magical ability. A typical wizard
might have an aspect relating them to a magical school or professional organization, or an independent wizard who trained
them. A character who is granted the ability to perform miracles by a divine patron would have obligations to that patron.
Maybe the power was granted by an artifact of great power, which still exerts some control over the character. Attachments
to these entities can come with some advantages, but they are also great opportunities to compel an aspect.

Some species have intrinsic magical ability. Characters belonging to one of these species can gain magic ability without
spending refresh by taking the relevant cultural stunt. Of the standard species, Elves (and Half-Elves that follow the
Elven destiny) can gain access to either Enchantment or Glamor (or both if they take the stunt twice), and Gnomes can
gain access to Technomancy. Characters with intrinsic magical ability can spend Refresh points for additional realms of
magic, but must have an aspect tied to magic use to do so.

## Using Magic
At the basic level, magic is an alternate way of achieving the same effect as a standard skill check. **Will** is used
instead of the skill normally used, except in the case of intrinsic magic, which uses **Culture**. The opposition is
determined the same way as it would be for any other skill check.
Examples:
- A character proficient in the Evocation realm wishes to shoot a firebolt at an enemy. This is handled the same way any
ranged weapon would be handled, except **Will** is used instead of **Shoot**, opposed by the target's **Athletics**. On
a success, the shifts are applied as physical damage.
- If the character wishes to target multiple opponents with a fireball, it is treated as an area effect weapon, with
the shifts divided among the targets.
- A divination used to gather information is treated the same way as an **Investigate** skill check.
- An illusion intended to mislead a target would be treated the same way as a **Deceive** skill check.

Situations in which a skill check would not be required would normally not require a skill check for magic, as long as
the character has proficiency in the realm of magic. Using magic to light a fire or levitate a spanner across the room
can be assumed to be within the basic competence of a trained practitioner of magic.

## More Power!
It will be apparent at this point that the beginning practitioner of magic is very limited in ability. There are some
distinct advantages to being able to do things with magic, such as not needing the usual tools to perform actions, but
for the most part a wizard at the beginning of their career is more style than anything else. There are plenty of people
in the *Pirates of Haven* setting that never progressed in their knowledge beyond the basics, but most people who put
in the work to learn magic are attracted to the power that comes with dedication to the craft. There are three ways to
do this, all of which involve spending refresh points.

### Expand Knowledge
This is the way of versatility. Each realm of magic must be learned separately, and each costs one point of refresh. Some
realms have subfields, and when first learned, the wizard gets access to two of those fields. Additional fields come with
spending additional refresh. A generalist wizard benefits from having a high **Will**, as that skill can be used in place
many other skills.

### Deepen Knowledge
Some wizards choose to specialize in one or two realms, spending refresh on stunts that increase their mastery of their
chosen fields.

### The Mana Track
The mana track is a measure of reserve power the practitioner can call on to increase spell effectiveness. Through study
and experience, a wizard learns efficiency and control, and can make greater use of the power at their disposal.

The mana track has some common characteristics of the physical and mental stress tracks, but works on different principals.
The mana track starts with zero boxes. For every two refresh spent on realm access or magic-related stunts, one box is added
to the track. The maximum size of the track is four boxes, though a high-powered campaign may set the limit higher.

When casting a spell successfully, a character may increase the spell's effectiveness by checking a box on the mana track.
A number of shifts corresponding to the box that was marked is added to the result. For example, checking the first box
would add +1 shift, which checking the fourth box on a full-length mana track would add +4 shifts. Only one box can be
check per spell.

Unlike the stress tracks, the mana track is not cleared at the end of a scene. Instead, one mark may be shifted down to
the next lower box, or cleared if in the lowest box. This shift occurs at the end of every scene whether or not magic is
used. For example, if the second and third box are checked, the check in the third box cannot be cleared because the second
box is already checked, so the check in the second box is moved to the first. After the next scene, if the mana track
has not been used, the player can either clear the first box or move the check in the third box down to the second.

A character can also use emergency mana, exceed the safe use of power when in a desperate situation. This is available
whether or not the mana track has any boxes. Instead of checking a box on the mana track, the character takes a consequence,
adding +1 shift for a mild consequence, +2 for moderate, or +3 for severe. This can be either a physical or a mental
consequence.

## Concentration
Some magic effects require concentration to achieve or maintain, such as divination rituals or persistent illusions. Unless
the environment is hostile or particularly distracting, concentration can be assumed (unless the GM thinks it would be
interesting to compel an aspect to break it). In a hostile or distracting environment, the character may have to work to
maintain concentration. The skill used to maintain concentration is **Discipline**. The following
situations may break concentration or require a skill check to maintain it:
- Taking stress: Take an Overcome action, using the amount of stress taken as the opposition.
- Taking a consequence: Automatically breaks concentration.
- Performing a demanding action: Anything that requires a skill roll other than the object of concentration requires an Overcome action, with the difficulty based on the complexity of the action.
- Hostile attempt to break concentration: Actions taken by a hostile party with the intent of breaking concentration, such as shouting (**Provoke**), shoving (**Physique**), or grappling (**Fight**) are Overcome actions opposed by **Discipline**.
- Scene aspects that are potentially distracting can be invoked against efforts to maintain concentration.
- When forgoing your action during an exchange for all-out defense, you may choose to take the +2 to **Discipline** checks to maintain concentration instead of Defend actions.
- Maintaining concentration for multiple effects adds a cumulative +2 to the difficulty for each additional effect.

## Effects on Technology
The more advanced a piece of technology is, the more it depends on the laws of physics to behave in a predictable manner.
When magic starts changing the way the laws of nature work, technology does not handle it well. The results can be anything
from the power blinking on and off to showers of sparks and blown circuits. This can happen one of two ways: accidentally
and deliberately.
### Accidental Hexing
Whether magic should affect nearby technology as an unintended side-effect depends mostly on what would be interesting. If
neither result has any bearing on the story, it doesn't matter and can be narrated in whatever way makes sense. If having
tech go haywire would be interesting, the next question is whether it would be a story complication. If not, go ahead and have
the tech act up. If it would complicate matters for the player characters, then it should be handled by compelling one of
the character's aspects - presumably the one that gives access to magic. In cases where the caster is attempting to be subtle, 
and the caster's level of control is relevant, take an Overcome action using **Discipline**. If the caster adds power to
the spell using the mana track, the same value is added to the difficulty of avoiding accidental hexing.
### Intentional Hexing
Being able to manipulate the laws that technology depends on can be a great advantage when your opponents are dependent on
technology. A hex is a spell cast merely for the side effect. Any spell will do, and so any magical practitioner can do it,
regardless of realm. The difficulty level depends on how advanced the technology is. The more advanced, the easier it is
to disrupt. The target piece of technology is treated as a character with a stress track. Most equipment has two boxes,
while something hardened against magic might have three or four, and an especially fragile item might have only one box,
or none. Stress can be reduced by taking a mild, moderate, or severe consequence; this affects repair time. Stress in excess
of available consequences results in destruction of the target. It item that only takes stress is disabled, but the stress
decreases by one every exchange (in the same way as the mana track) until it comes back online, It automatically loses all
stress at the end of the scene.

A Guide to Hexing Difficulty

| Intentional    | Avoiding      | Tech Type                     |
|----------------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| Legendary (+8) | Terrible (-2) | Purely mechanical             |
| Fantastic (+6) | Mediocre (0)  | Early industrial              |
| Great (+4)     | Average (+1)  | Early electronics             |
| Good (+3)      | Fair (+2)     | Legacy tech still in wide use |
| Fair (+2)      | Good (+3)     | Most contemporary tech        |
| Average (+1)   | Great (+4)    | Leading-edge innovations      |
| Mediocre (+0)  | Superb (+5)   | Revolutionary prototype tech  |

### Species considerations
Elves and Half-Elves that choose the Elven destiny have this effect on technology simply by being near it, regardless of
any ability to use magic, and so are a constant threat to technology. This does not grant the ability to cast intentional
hexes, even for Elves that have cultural stunts giving them innate magical abilities. Elves who acquire magical abilities
through spending refresh on realm access can cast hexes as any other spellcaster.

Gnomes do not have any effect on technology through their use of magic, either intentionally or unintentionally, but may
duplicate the effects of an intentional hex by using technomancy to sabotage equipment.

## Magic Suppression
Along with the ability to manipulate the forces of magic to defy the laws of nature comes the ability to strengthen those
laws, exerting the will to prevent other from manipulating them. When a character spends a refresh point to gain access
to a magical realm, they also gain **Reinforce Reality** as a free stunt. The Reinforce Reality stunt allows the character
to use **Will** to create the *Reality Reinforced* aspect in a scene, with a free invoke as normal (two if succeeding with
style). In addition, for every two shifts on the skill check to create the aspect, there is an additional +1 to all opposition
to magic, regardless of whether the aspect is invoked or not. The mana track may be used to increase the number of shifts.
The effect applies to all magic with the affected zone, with no regard for friend or foe. The effect remains while the character
concentrates, and a character may not use magic of any kind while reinforcing reality. The difficulty of suppressing magic
in one zone is usually Fair (+2), with each additional zone increasing the difficulty by +2.

## Realms of Magic
The way magic is conceptualized and categorized can vary with culture and magical tradition. What follows is suggested as
a basic approach. Some magical traditions might include niche realms, such as chronomancy or blood magic, or organize magic
differently. Each realm of power gives some examples of how they can be used for various actions. Defensive magic requires
preparation, which is done with a Create Advantage action rather than a Defend action. The defensive aspect created is then
invoked on the Defense roll.

- **Abjuration:** Creating barriers and shields, dispelling magical effects, banishing supernatural creatures
  - Overcome: dispel magical effect, neutralize curse
  - Create Advantage: shield, sanctified ground
  - Attack: banish creature (creates mental stress). The type of creature that can be banished depends on magical
  tradition, but examples include extraplanar, undead, evil, and fey. 
- **Divination:** Gathering information about the past or present. Divination involving more than a few seconds into the
future is murky. Arcanists disagree about why, some saying that it is more dependable than not, others claiming that this
is selection bias. One prevailing opinion is that knowing what would have happened changes the probability. Your mileage
may vary. Divination works much like the **Investigate** skill, using a different mechanism.
  - Overcome: locate a hidden person or object, learn past events
  - Create Advantage: find useful information about someone, create a cover story
- Elemental: Controlling primal matter and energy. Categorization of elements is dependent on culture and magical tradition. The classical
elements in Tanrith society are earth, air, fire, and water. For the Cho Ji they are wood, fire, earth, metal, and water. The
Elves of Ciniar they are earth, wood, light, water, air, and spirit. Dwarves often distinguish between water and ice, while
Gnomes usually include electricity and force. Elemental matter that is conjured is unstable and dissolves and returns to
its plane of origin when the scene ends, though side effects remain. For example, elemental fire can form a barrier wall
and burn with no fuel, but can also ignite anything combustible that gets too close. At the end of the scene, the wall of
fire is gone, but anything that it ignited continues to burn.
  - Overcome: move something with a gust of wind, burn/melt/freeze a door
  - Create Advantage: barrier of flame/ice/air, strong wind, strong/weak gravity
  - Attack: bolt of fire/lightning/ice, crushing gravity, heat armor
- **Enchantment:** Influencing minds, particularly emotions and mental state. Interacting with the intellect or conscious
thought is the realm of **Mental** magic. Enchantment is used like the **Rapport** and **Provoke** skills. It can also be
used to assess emotions like the **Empathy** skill.
  - Overcome: get past a guard, earn someone's confidence, put someone to sleep
  - Create Advantage: create an emotional state such as *Inspired* or *Anxious*, learn someone's fears or motivations
  - Attack: direct mental attack on an opponent
- **Glamor:** Creating sensory input. The sensory input is real, and can fool electronic sensors as well as
organic beings. Static illusions do not require concentration, but if the illusion needs to move or
otherwise change, the caster must maintain concentration for the effect to continue.
  - Overcome: false credentials, mislead or hide from observers
  - Create Advantage: invisibility, disguise, distract
- **Mental:** Influencing memory and thought. Emotional affects use the realm of **Enchantment**.
  - Overcome: read minds, alter/erase memories, communicate mind-to-mind
  - Create Advantage: implant suggestion, disorient opponent 
  - Attack direct mental attack on an opponent
- **Movement:** Moving objects or self. This includes changing the speed of movement, but all movement has to be
has to be contiguous. THere is no known move between non-connected points, also known as teleportation. Affecting
speed allows movement through additional zones as sprinting, but does not give multiple actions during an exchange.
  - Overcome: telekinesis, levitation, flying, moving into or out of astral space, sprinting
  - Create Advantage: increased or decreased speed
  - Attack: hurl objects at target, slam target into a wall
- **Nature:** Affecting flora, fauna, and natural environments.
  - Overcome: grow plants to cover tracks, summon animal for a mount, calm beast
  - Create Advantage: assistance from woodland creatures, grow vines for climbing handholds, entangle opponents with plant growth
- **Necromancy:** Caommanding life, death, and undeath
  - Overcome: speak with the dead, animate zombie
  - Create Advantage: cause opponents to feel their mortality, exert control of undead
  - Attack: drain vitality
- **Summoning:** Calling extra-planar and magical entities. Also including binding and controlling.
  - Overcome: summon extra-planar being, bind creature to caster's will, negotiate for service
  - Create Advantage: know weakness of summoned creature
- **Technomancy:** Interfacing with or manipulating technology
  - Overcome: access system, sabotage, control remotely
  - Create Advantage: disrupt sensors, boost performance
- **Transformation:** Changing the form or nature of beings or objects. The transformation mimics the generic form of
the object, but not a specific instance. So you could transform a screwdriver into a key card, but not into the
specific key card that would get you into the manager's office. The transformation does not last beyond the
end of the scene, so creating forgeries of antiquities is not a very good racket, since you would not have
time to make the getaway.
  - Overcome: become a literal fly on the wall, change a tool into the one that's needed, shrink a captive to fit into
  your pocket
  - Create Advantage: turn the floor to mud, gain claws, make your skin as hard as durasteel


## Example of Magic Use
***
The renegade wizard Arakor has been seen on a space station in border space. A fire team of four Yrth marines has been sent
to apprehend him, accompanied by the Imperial Wizard Valen. Arakor has been alerted to their presence -- one does not survive
long as a renegade wizard without taking precautions -- and takes defensive measures. His first thought is to make himself
invisible and create aan illusion of himself a couple meters away. The GM desides this effect is not complicated, and assigns
a difficulty of Fair (+2), but rules that these are two different effects, each of which require concentration. Arakor's
player thinks instead about bending light to make him appear to be in a different location, which would not incur the extra
+2 modifier to **Disicpline** checks for maintaining concentration with multiple effects. The GM decides that this is more
difficult, given to need to make the displacement look correct for multiple observers, and assigns a difficulty of Great (+4).
Arakor has a **Discipline** of Superb (+5), and decides he can handle the extra load. Those who are unwilling to take risks
do not typically become renegade wizards. Arakor also has a high will, Fantastic (+6). With a roll of +3, he succeeds with
style, creating the aspect *You Can't See Me*, with two free invokes. He then rolls +1 on the second effect, again succeeding
with style, creating the aspect *I'm Over Here*, again with two free invokes.  Because he was already  concentrating on one
effect while casting the spell, he must make an Overcome action using **Discipline** to maintain the invisibility. The GM
decides that this is pretty straight-forward, with no active opposition yet, and assigns a difficulty of Average (+1).
Arakor's player rolls -3, for a total of +2 and maintains his concentration. Now concentrating on an additional effect,
subsequent **Discipline** checks will add +2 to the difficulty.

As they approach, Valen reinforces reality to prevent Arakor from attacking with magic and to keep the marines' blaster
rifles functioning. Valen's **Will** is Great (+4) and rolls +1 on the dice for a total of +5 against a passive difficulty
of Fair (+2), for a total of three shifts. Valen succeeds with style, and the *Reality Reinforced* aspect is added to the scene,
Valen getting two free invokes. In addition, all opposition to magic receives a +1, which is half of the shifts rounded down.
Valen's player decides to check the first box on his mana track to add another shift, and increase the magic opposition to +2.
Valen gives the signal, and the marines use their action to move into the zone where Arakor is.

As the marines approach, Arakor feels the shift as Valen's will reinforces reality. Confident in his ability, he attempts
to disable some of the marines' blaster rifles. Though they are military-grade, the underlying technology is not especially
technologically advanced, giving a difficulty of Fair (+2), but the magic suppression raises it to Great (+4). Arakor rolls +2,
resulting in four shifts and success with style. Knowing that he is going to need the backing of the marines and their weapons,
Valen uses one of his free invokes to increase the opposition by two, and Arakor has only two shifts to spend. Rather than
disable one rifle for two exchanges, he chooses to split the shifts disable two rifles for the next exchange. Arakor must now
roll to maintain concentration. Hexes don't require much attention, so the GM assigns a difficulty of Mediocre (+0), which
becomes Fair (+2) because he is concentrating on multiple things. With a roll of 0, for a total of +5, he passes easily.

Because of the importance of suppressing magic, Valen's next action is an all-out defense, taking +2 to **Discipline** checks
to maintain concentration. Arakor then does something entirely unexpected -- he takes out a blaster pistol and shoots
Valen. He has an unusually high **Shoot** skill for a wizard, at Good (+3). He rolls +0, for a total of 3 against Valen's
**athletics** skill of Average (+1) and a roll of -1, for a total of three shifts. Wanting to make it count, Arakor, player
invokes his **Never Do What They Expect** aspect, for a total of five shifts. With only two boxes on his physical stress
track, Valen is forced to take a moderate consequence (Bad Burn) to avoid being taken out, and more importantly, his concentration
is broken. Arakor maintains his concentration against a total difficulty of Great (+4), rolls a 0 for a total of +5, and keeps
the illusions in place.

The four marines fire at Arakor. Two of the blaster rifles fail to do anything, but will be operational again for the next
exchange. The marines have a shoot skill of Great (+4). THe first rolls -2, for a total of 2. Arakor has an **Athletics**
of Fair (+2). He rolls -1, for a total of 1. He uses one of his free invokes of *You Can't See Me* to raise his defense roll
to 3, and the shot misses. The other marine rolls +4, for a total of 8. Arakor also rolls 4, for a total of 6. He uses a free
invoke of *I'm over here* to tie. The marine invokes his *Pharm-Enhanced Senses* aspect to succeed by 2. Arakor uses the
remaining free invoke on *You Can't See Me*, glad that he opted to test his concentration for the extra aspect. The tie does
no damage, and Arakor does not have to check to maintain concentration. The marine get the boost *I Heard Something Over There*.

Valen finds magic suppression to be of less use against an opponent armed with a tech weapon, and instead unleashes a blast
of fire at Arakor. Rolling a +2 brings the attack to +6. Arakor again rolls -1 for defense, for a result of five shifts on the
attack. He uses his final free invoke of *I'm Over Here*, as well as his free invoke of the consequence he gave Valen, to
bring it to +1. Valen checks the second box on his mana track to pour more power into the spell and give him a +3. Arakor
chooses not to spend his only fate point to invoke another aspect, and marks the third box on his physical stress track.
He now needs to make a **Discipline** check to maintain his concentration. He rolls poorly, a -2, for a total of +3, against
a target of +3, for the stress taken, +2 for concentrating on an additional effect. The illusions fail, and everybody can
see where he is standing. Valen also needs to make a **Discipline** check to see if he hexed any nearby tech with his fire
spell. For standard tech the target is Good (+3), but his use of the second box on the mana track adds another +2 to the
difficulty, for a total of +5. With a **Discipline** of Good (+3), he rolls a -1, for a total of +2, and fails. The lights
in the corridor go dark in a shower of sparks, and the marines once again lose the use of their blasters.

Arakor manages to escape in the ensuing chaos.
